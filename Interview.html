Cindy on AI, Art, and Refusal

A freshman at CalArts, an illustrator doing anime-centered commissions, and someone living between experimental art and commercial polish. This page stages that tension as a series of unfolding questions.

Q01 As a freshman at CalArts and someone doing illustration commissions, how would you describe your current relationship to art-making? +

For me, it feels like a kind of split personality in my art practice.

When I’m doing CalArts work, I’m more interested in “doing art” in the sense of making something abstract, experimental, or conceptually loose. But when I’m working on commissions, my style becomes much more commercial and more refined.

Since most of my commission work is for otaku audiences—people who really love Japanese anime—the visual language is completely different from what I make at school. Sometimes when my classmates see that work, they’re genuinely shocked that I drew it.

Q02 In the illustration field, AI is often completely banned. Why is that boundary so strict? +

It’s very simple: at least in the art field, AI and artists are in a zero-sum game. If AI becomes powerful enough, artists will inevitably lose their jobs. I don’t think there’s any middle ground right now.

People like to say AI is just a tool that helps artists make rough sketches or generate inspiration. But the reality is that with only a few keywords, AI can already produce a complete, hyper-detailed image almost instantly. Some systems can even mimic the work of master artists extremely well.

So then what is the human doing in that process? Just typing?

Q03 Is that ban really about artistic integrity, or is it more about protecting artists’ labor and income? +

All I can say is that this rule feels like an unspoken pact among artists.

On the surface, people talk about integrity. But underneath that, it’s really a shared consensus among creators: we cannot let an alien species take over all of our niches.

It’s basically the same thing. AI is like an invasive species with no natural predators. It has completely disrupted the ecosystem of artists. So artists have no choice but to try to forcibly keep this Pandora’s box sealed, preventing it from being fully opened.

Q04 When clients commission you, are they paying for the image itself, or for the fact that you created it? +

I think the people willing to pay for my work are people with refined aesthetic taste—lol.

In our circle, AI is strongly opposed, so anyone who uses it—artist or client—will be disliked just as much. The social cost of using AI is very high.

I also think commissioning art is not that different from buying luxury goods. Both are driven by vanity, by the desire to show off, to prove you have money and good taste. Using AI in that context is like carrying a fake Louis Vuitton bag. People will absolutely look down on you for it.

Q05 The game industry seems more tolerant of AI. Why do you think the rules loosen there? +

I think it’s because a lot of gamers don’t care that much about graphics, especially in games like League of Legends where gameplay matters more.

But even then, AI is still a huge sticking point in games that depend heavily on visuals. A lot of well-known Chinese game companies I know of, like NetEase and Tencent, have already been criticized by players over suspected AI-related incidents.

Games that rely on selling characters and skins will face a full-scale player boycott the moment they openly use AI, and that reaction becomes even stronger in communities that care deeply about visual design.

Q06 Do you see this as a double standard between independent artists and large studios? +

Not really. I don’t think the issue is whether it’s an individual or a studio. What matters more is how closely that industry is tied to art itself.

The stronger that connection is, the more likely AI use will backfire. Even if certain game or art companies start using AI in the future, they’ll have to do it discreetly. They absolutely cannot let players find out.

If it’s too obvious, they’ll get slammed. That part is certain.

Q07 If studios use AI to generate or speed up work, does that change how you value the final product—or your own future in that field? +

Honestly, I don’t really dare to think about it too much. Hahaha.

I don’t think AI can completely replace humans, but what about 70%? That feels very possible. Maybe only the top 30% of the most talented or influential artists will remain, while the rest—the ones whose work can be fully substituted by AI—will really be affected.

And who can guarantee they won’t be among that 70%?

Q08 When you heard CalArts was introducing an AI course, what was your immediate reaction? +

To be honest, while I completely understand why so many people at our school are resisting the course, I still think taking an AI course is essential.

Bluntly speaking, people who know nothing about AI will eventually be left behind by the times. There’s an old saying: know yourself and know your enemy, and you will never be defeated in a hundred battles. I think that applies now too.

Only by actually understanding what AI is can you identify your own irreplaceable value in this era—things like creativity or a unique style. At least for now, I still believe AI cannot create its own real artistic style.

That said, I’m not a fan of AI myself, because I honestly think AI-generated images are ugly. No matter how detailed they are, using that kind of trash to fob the audience off feels disrespectful.

Q09 Why protest the course instead of engaging with it? What does that say about how urgent this issue feels? +

That goes without saying. CalArts is probably one of the few schools in the U.S. with such a strong artistic atmosphere and such a high degree of freedom.

I think the students’ resistance comes from the fear that if CalArts—a pioneer and a role model in the arts—starts openly offering AI courses, then maybe that means the whole industry has really gone to the dogs.

A lot of the anger is directed at the school itself: for damaging its own reputation, and for behaving irresponsibly.

Q10 Do you think the school presents AI as a neutral tool, without fully addressing its ethical implications? +

To be honest, I’m not entirely sure, because I never actually took the class.

So I can’t say for certain how the school frames it. But from the intensity of the reaction, I think a lot of students are worried that the institutional framing itself already normalizes AI too much, especially at a school with symbolic weight like CalArts.

Q11 As someone navigating both commissions and industry expectations, do you feel caught between a system that bans AI and one that increasingly depends on it? +

My current situation isn’t really that contradictory. Both my school environment and the commission community I’m in hate AI.

But that doesn’t mean I haven’t been affected by it. Unfortunately, most people in society who don’t care about art also don’t care whether the posters, ads, or images they see on the street were generated by AI.

This is extremely widespread in China. Every time I ride the subway or just walk outside, my eyes are bombarded with AI-generated imagery that I find absolutely hideous—and that’s what makes me feel most hopeless.

I’ve realized that no matter how much people with good taste resist AI, it still ends up everywhere, filling every street and alley.

Q12 Looking ahead, do you see yourself working with AI, resisting it, or constantly negotiating between the two? +

Like I said, the main reason I hate AI is that I see it as a form of cheating—a way of cutting corners. It deceives the client’s eyes, and it feels like a dereliction of duty toward oneself.

Every time I see some company using AI-generated images and plastering them all over the internet or across billboards, I just want to ask: do you take me for a fool? You’re trying to fool me with this trash that takes only a few seconds to generate, and you still expect to make money off me?

Of course, I can’t guarantee I’ll never use AI in the future. Humans have always had to deal with things they hate in order to survive. It’s been that way since the dawn of time.
